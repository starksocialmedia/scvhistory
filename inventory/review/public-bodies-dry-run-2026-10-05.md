## Santa Clarita Community College District

school/district, level valley, role governs. Founded November 21, 1967.

The Santa Clarita Community College District is the public body that runs College of the Canyons, the valley's community college. Its main campus is in Valencia, at 26455 Rockwell Canyon Road, and a second campus, on 70 acres at 17200 Sierra Highway in Canyon Country, opened for classes on August 27, 2007.[1][2][3] The district lies wholly within Los Angeles County and serves an area of 367 square miles.[2] A board of five trustees elected by the voters governs it. Since 2016 each trustee has been elected by the voters of one of five trustee areas of roughly equal population, and must live in that area; elections fall in November of even-numbered years.[4][17]

Valley voters approved a junior college district on November 21, 1967, and elected its first board the same day: William G. Bonelli Jr., Bruce Fortine, Sheila Dyer, Peter Huntsinger and Edward Muhl.[5] The board began to meet in December.[6] On the evening of Wednesday, December 13, it named the district the Santa Clarita Valley Junior College District, over objections, at a time when the valley itself had no official name.[7] The trustees named the college College of the Canyons on May 15, 1969.[8] "Valley" was later dropped from the district's name, and it took its present name in 1972, when California's junior colleges became community colleges.[9]

The college opened in the fall of 1969 with about 800 students, in the evenings, at Hart High School in Newhall, under Dr. Robert C. Rockwell, the district's first superintendent-president.[10] Voters approved a $4 million building bond in January 1970; the district bought 153.4 acres in Valencia, and the first classes there met in portable buildings on October 5, 1970.[11][12] A second bond, of $8 million, passed on February 6, 1973, and Governor Ronald Reagan dedicated the first permanent building, the Instructional Resource Center named for Bonelli, on April 22, 1974.[12] Bonelli had died in office in February 1972, and his seat was filled at a special election that June.[13] Dr. Dianne G. Van Hook became superintendent-president on July 1, 1988, and the college's later histories call her chancellor.[18]

For most of its history the trustees held numbered seats and were elected at large by every voter in the district, in odd-numbered years.[14][19] In 2015, to settle a lawsuit under the California Voting Rights Act brought by Jim Soliz and Rosemarie Sanchez-Fraser, who argued that at-large voting was a barrier to Latino participation, the board agreed to elect its trustees by area.[15] It adopted five trustee areas on November 4, 2015, and sent them to the Board of Governors of the California Community Colleges for approval in January 2016.[16] The same change moved the district's elections to even-numbered years, and the first election by area was held on November 8, 2016.[14][17]

## Santa Clarita Valley Sanitation District

government, level valley, role administers. Founded 2005.

The Santa Clarita Valley Sanitation District collects and treats the valley's sewage. It serves the City of Santa Clarita and the unincorporated areas around it, Stevenson Ranch and Castaic among them, about 250,000 residents and businesses, through more than 42 miles of trunk sewers, one pumping plant and two treatment plants, the Saugus and Valencia water reclamation plants.[1] The local sewers that feed its trunk lines are the City's or the County's.[1] It is one of the 24 districts of the Los Angeles County Sanitation Districts, formed under the County Sanitation District Act of 1923, which share one staff at Whittier under a joint administration agreement while each keeps its own board. Its board is the Mayor of Santa Clarita, a second member of the City Council, and the chair of the County Board of Supervisors.[1]

The Saugus plant, on Springbrook Avenue, began operating in 1962. It was enlarged in 1964, 1965 and 1968, given tertiary filters in 1987, and brought to its design capacity of 6.5 million gallons a day in 1991; it handles no solids, which go by trunk sewer to Valencia.[2] The Valencia plant, on 27 acres on The Old Road west of Interstate 5, opened in 1967 with a capacity of 1.5 million gallons a day and was enlarged to 21.6 million by 2003. It processes the solids from both plants.[3]

The district is the union of two older ones. District No. 26, formed in 1962, served the eastern half of the valley from the Saugus plant; District No. 32, formed in 1965, served the western half from Valencia. When the east's sewage outgrew the Saugus plant, which its site would not let it enlarge, a pipeline carried the excess to Valencia, and in 1984 the two districts signed an agreement to share the system and its costs. They were consolidated as the Santa Clarita Valley Sanitation District in 2005.[1]

The plants discharge treated water to the Santa Clara River under permits with chloride limits. The Regional Water Quality Control Board set an objective of 100 milligrams per liter for the river, to protect salt-sensitive crops downstream, and the plants do not remove chloride.[4][5] The district went first after household water softeners: voters approved Measure S on November 4, 2008, enacting the Santa Clara River Chloride Reduction Ordinance of 2008, which required residential automatic water softeners to be removed by June 30, 2009. More than 8,000 were taken out, but the plants still could not meet the limit.[4][6] In October 2013 the board approved a chloride compliance project and certified its environmental impact report. A court set the report aside in 2016; the board certified a recirculated report on August 30, 2017, and the litigation over it ended in February 2022.[5] Ultraviolet disinfection began at both plants in 2021, and an advanced treatment plant using reverse osmosis at Valencia in October 2023, its brine trucked to the Districts' plant in Carson.[5][3]

## Los Angeles County Department of Regional Planning

government, level county, role administers, under County of Los Angeles. Founded 1974.

The Department of Regional Planning is the County's planning department for every part of Los Angeles County outside a city, and so for Castaic, Stevenson Ranch, Val Verde, Agua Dulce and the rest of the unincorporated valley. It writes the long-range plans, reviews development projects and answers zoning complaints.[1][2] It began as the staff of the Regional Planning Commission, founded in 1923, and became an independent County department in 1974.[1]

Its plan for the valley is the Santa Clarita Valley Area Plan, a part of the County General Plan that applies only to the unincorporated valley. The Board of Supervisors adopted it on November 27, 2012, and it took effect on December 27. It is the County's half of "One Valley One Vision," a joint planning effort with the City of Santa Clarita, whose General Plan, the other half, the City Council had adopted on June 14, 2011.[3] It replaced the County's area plan of 1984, revised in 1990.[4][5] The department is the lead agency for the environmental review of the County's projects in the valley, as for the NorthLake Specific Plan near Castaic in 2017 and Tesoro del Valle in 2018.[6][5]

## Los Angeles County Regional Planning Commission

government, level county, role advises, under County of Los Angeles. Founded 1923.

The Regional Planning Commission is the Board of Supervisors' advisory body on planning for the unincorporated county, the unincorporated Santa Clarita Valley among it. Its five commissioners are appointed by the Board to four-year terms; they hold public hearings and administer the State Planning Law, the Subdivision Map Act, the California Environmental Quality Act and the County's zoning and subdivision ordinances.[1] It was founded in 1923, and the County's planning department grew out of its staff.[2]

Its hearings have shaped much of the valley outside the city. In October 1964 it heard the zone change for Princess Park Estates in the Valencia Valley, where slides early in 1969 forced the demolition of a number of homes.[3] It recommended to the Board the plan amendment for Skyline Ranch in Canyon Country, whose environmental report the Board certified on December 7, 2010.[4] In 2005 applicants for land-use permits in Agua Dulce told the Superior Court that County staff, the Commission or the Board had sent them to present their projects to the Agua Dulce Town Council before the County would consider them further.[5]

## Castaic Area Town Council

nonprofit, level none, role advises. Founded not given.

The Castaic Area Town Council is a nonprofit corporation whose ten directors are elected by Castaic's residents, two from each of five regions, to speak for the unincorporated community to the County. It reviews land-use applications and other proposals before County Planning and works with the Board of Supervisors and County departments such as Regional Planning and Parks and Recreation.[1][2] It is not a government. Its bylaws make it "a voluntary organization of elected members who receive no remuneration," bar it from borrowing, and give its purposes as presenting "community concerns to appropriate agencies" and reviewing "public or private proposals that may affect the community."[1] The Internal Revenue Service lists it as a 501(c)(3) organization, recognized in 2001.[3]

Its bylaws were adopted on July 19, 2000. They set its area as that of the Castaic Union School District, with the Peter Pitchess Honor Rancho east of Castaic Creek, and its directors' terms at four years, no more than two in a row. A director must be an adult, a registered voter, and a resident of his or her region for a year; elections were set for the first Tuesday in November, by secret ballot under an election committee.[1] Half its seats are now filled every two years.[2] The County treats it as the community's voice: its notice for the Tapia Ranch project of 2007 lists the "Castaic Town Council" among the special reviewing agencies.[4]

## Agua Dulce Town Council

nonprofit, level none, role advises. Founded September 21, 1991.

The Agua Dulce Town Council speaks for Agua Dulce, an unincorporated town, to the County and other governments. Its seven members are elected by Agua Dulce's registered voters to two-year terms, in an election each November run by its own election committee. It reviews public and private proposals that may affect the community and gives the County its recommendation on land-use applications there.[1][2][3] It cannot make law: its bylaws say it "cannot pass laws, and cannot levy assessments or taxes," and that its charter gives it no authority over anyone except that it "is the only group that can speak for the community."[2] The Internal Revenue Service lists it as a 501(c)(4) organization.[4]

Agua Dulce's residents created it by approving its charter on September 21, 1991, and it was incorporated as a nonprofit public benefit corporation in 1994.[1][3] In April 2005 it voted that its meetings were not to be recorded by outsiders, and residents sued. Ruling on August 1, 2005, the Superior Court held that the unincorporated town is a "local agency" under the Brown Act and the council, created by charter and serving "in an advisory capacity to the community of Agua Dulce and to the County of Los Angeles," a legislative body bound by it. The council had rescinded the ban in May, so the case was dismissed.[3] The court recorded applicants' testimony that County staff, the Regional Planning Commission or the Board of Supervisors had sent them to the council first.[3] With the Acton Town Council it opposed the Cemex sand and gravel mine in Soledad Canyon, and when the Board of Supervisors approved the mine's permit in 2004 the two councils were each given a seat on its community advisory committee.[5]

## Consolidated Fire Protection District of Los Angeles County

government, level county, role administers, under County of Los Angeles. Founded 1949.

The Consolidated Fire Protection District of Los Angeles County, run as the County of Los Angeles Fire Department and governed by the Board of Supervisors, provides fire protection and emergency medical service to the City of Santa Clarita and to all of the unincorporated valley.[1][2] The City is inside the district rather than a customer of it: the department lists Santa Clarita among the 47 cities whose service is paid for by a share of their property taxes and a voter-approved special tax, apart from the thirteen cities it bills under fee-for-service contracts.[1] Fifteen of its stations serve the valley, numbered 73, 76, 104, 107, 108, 111, 123, 124, 126, 128, 132, 143, 149, 150 and 156, from Newhall, Valencia, Canyon Country, Stevenson Ranch and Castaic; Station 126 on Citrus Street is the Battalion 6 headquarters, Station 150 on Golden Valley Road the Battalion 22 headquarters, and Station 76 in Valencia one of the County's three hazardous materials stations.[2][3] The newest permanent station, 104 on Golden Valley Road, was begun on August 9, 2018, to replace a temporary one.[4]

Before the district, the County's Department of Forester and Fire Warden guarded the valley.[5][1] Its Soledad Division office opened in Newhall in July 1923 with two paid men, and its headquarters station on San Fernando Road was built in the winter of 1928; in 1940 the division covered 1,014 square miles from the Santa Clara Divide to the Kern County line, answered 230 fires in 1939, and kept sixteen men at Newhall.[5] The same year the Soledad office opened, the Newhall Chamber of Commerce set out to form a fire district for the town: in May 1923 it named a committee to organize one, in September its chairman, P.C. Miller, reported that the Board of Supervisors had accepted the petition, and in October he reported that no money for apparatus would be available before the next March.[6][7][8] A Station 73 captain, Paul Peppard, wrote in 2014 that a Newhall Fire Protection District was formed in 1953 and shared the Newhall station with the forestry crews, engine 73 for the wildland and engine 273 for the town, until the two merged in 1954.[9] The County dates the Consolidated Fire Protection District to 1949, made from fire districts that had existed since the 1920s, and in 1992 the district annexed all the county's remaining unincorporated territory and took over the Forester and Fire Warden's duties.[1] Station 73 was dedicated in July 1958 beside the 1928 headquarters, which it replaced.[10] At the new City's first meeting, on December 15, 1987, the Council designated the County Fire Chief as City Fire Chief, and on June 23, 1988 it approved its service agreements with the County, the fire services contracts among them.[11][12]

## County of Los Angeles Public Library

government, level county, role administers, under County of Los Angeles. Founded 1912.

The County of Los Angeles Public Library, the county's library system since 1912, serves the unincorporated valley from three branches: the Castaic Library on Sloan Canyon Road, opened in November 2008 and enlarged in March 2015; the Stevenson Ranch Library on The Old Road, which began as an express library in July 2011 and became a full library in March 2015; and the Acton Agua Dulce Library on Crown Valley Road in Acton, opened September 29, 2010 for Acton and Agua Dulce.[1][2][3][4] Before the Castaic branch, the outlying communities, Val Verde, Agua Dulce, Acton, Castaic and Friendly Valley among them, were served by the County's Santa Clarita bookmobile.[2][5] Inside the City of Santa Clarita the libraries have been the City's own since July 1, 2011.[6][7]

The County's library came to the valley on March 1, 1916, when a Newhall County Library opened in Robert and Christine Woodard's ice cream parlor, with Christine Woodard as its custodian at $5 a year; it moved into a small building of its own in 1923.[8][3] Its Newhall branch librarian in 1940, Mary F. Brunner, compiled the first comprehensive history of the valley.[9] A new Newhall branch at 22704 Ninth Street was dedicated on November 18, 1957, with Rose Buddell as branch librarian.[10] The second permanent branch, at 18536 Soledad Canyon Road in Canyon Country, opened on September 20, 1971, replacing the Saugus Mobilibrary that the County had run with the Saugus Union School District, and the Valencia Library was dedicated on May 24, 1972.[11][8] The City incorporated in 1987 with the three branches still the County's, and in April 1988 the Council agreed to the County Librarian's request to put City signs on them.[12] The City paid more than $5 million toward the County-operated Canyon Country Jo Anne Darcy Library at 18601 Soledad Canyon Road, which opened on November 17, 2001.[13] By 2010 the County served the valley with four libraries, at Canyon Country, Valencia, Newhall and Castaic, and a bookmobile, until the City withdrew its three from the County system on July 1, 2011.[5][6][7]

## Santa Clarita Public Library

government, level valley, role administers, under The City of Santa Clarita. Founded July 1, 2011.

The Santa Clarita Public Library is the City of Santa Clarita's library, with three branches: the Canyon Country Jo Anne Darcy Library, the Old Town Newhall Library and the Valencia Library.[1] It began on July 1, 2011, when the City took over the three County libraries inside its limits, and it has been run by City staff since July 1, 2018.[2][3] Under the state's Municipal Libraries Act it is managed by a Board of Library Trustees.[2] The unincorporated valley is served by the County's own branches at Castaic and Stevenson Ranch.[4][5]

The City Council voted on August 24, 2010, after a public hearing with 557 written comments and a petition, to withdraw from the Los Angeles County Free Library System and set up a municipal library, and in the same motion gave its management to a private firm, Library Systems & Services, LLC, under a contract through June 30, 2016; the vote was four to one, Bob Kellar against.[6] That October the Council created a citizens' advisory committee, appointing 37 members in November, and on July 12, 2011 adopted the library's first strategic plan.[7] The branches reopened as a City system over the Fourth of July weekend of 2011 with LSSI's employees as their staff, after two lawsuits against the change had been dismissed.[8][2] The City opened the Old Town Newhall Library on Lyons Avenue on September 29, 2012, replacing the 1957 County branch on Ninth Street, which had passed to the City in July 2011 and was closed.[9][10][11] On January 9, 2018 the Council voted unanimously to end the LSSI contract and staff the libraries with City employees from July 1, 2018, judging that the state's 2012 pension reform made that the cheaper course and estimating a first-year saving of $393,931; 54 LSSI employees then staffed the branches.[2][12][13]
