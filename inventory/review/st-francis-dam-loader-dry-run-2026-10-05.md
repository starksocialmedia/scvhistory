The St. Francis Dam stood at the head of San Francisquito Canyon, above Saugus. The City of Los Angeles built it to store an emergency water supply, as a unit of the Los Angeles Aqueduct, and completed it in May 1926: a concrete dam 185 feet high.[1][2][5] It failed on the night of March 12, 1928, and its flood ran 54 miles down the Santa Clara River Valley to the sea.[1][2]

## (b) Event: St. Francis Dam Disaster

The St. Francis Dam stood at the head of San Francisquito Canyon, above Saugus. The City of Los Angeles had completed it about two years before, under its chief engineer, William Mulholland, to store an emergency water supply.[1][2] It failed in the last minutes of March 12, 1928: at 11:57:30 p.m. by the archive's photograph captions, "on or about 11:30 P.M." by the Newhall Land and Farming Company's report of March 24.[3][1] The water ran down the canyon to the Santa Clara River at Castaic Junction and west through Piru, Fillmore, Santa Paula, Saticoy and Ventura, and reached the ocean at 5:25 a.m. on March 13, 54 miles from the dam.[3]

The first miles of that path were this valley's. A mile and a half below the dam the flood swept away Powerhouse No. 2 and the homes of its workers. Alan Pollack counts three survivors there: Lillian Curtis, her son Danny, and the utility man Ray Rising. Lillian lost her husband, Lyman, and their daughters, Marjorie and Mazie.[4][5] Lower in the canyon the water killed members of the Ruiz family, who were buried in the family's cemetery, and destroyed the trading post and part of Harry Carey's ranch.[6][7] At Castaic Junction it took the highway and railroad bridges, the Edison company's transformer station and its workers' cottages, a gasoline station whose operator and one of his sons were lost, and an auto camp; the Newhall Land report of March 24 found "not a vestige of any building whatsoever" there but the concrete platform of the old section house.[8] Beyond the junction it overturned the Southern Pacific's track and struck the Edison construction camp at Kemp, by the Los Angeles-Ventura county line, where Pollack counts 84 workers and the night watchman, Ed Locke, among the dead.[9][10] Newhall Land counted 1,720 acres of its ranch totally destroyed.[11] Newhall itself was spared. "The town of Newhall was not harmed in any way," William S. Hart wrote, "but at one time there were 78 bodies in the little shack that had been converted into a morgue": the Masonic hall at Railroad Avenue and Market Street, where volunteers were setting up slabs by 4 a.m. on March 13.[12][13] Hart's ranch home served as a center for relief work.[14] Seven members of the Saugus Community Club were among the dead.[15]

How many died has been counted many times. Ann Stansell, a graduate student at California State University, Northridge, compiled a roster of the victims by name from 2011 to 2014; in his note of March 12, 2014, Leon Worden gave its total as "431 individuals": 306 recovered bodies, 240 of them identified, and 125 missing persons.[16] On January 17, 2018, he revised the figure on the same page: "the number of victims stands at 411," twenty fewer than the original list. "Each of the 20 had been reported missing in 1928, but no claim had been filed." Stansell had found all twenty in the 1930 census. "They survived."[17] This record gives 411, the archive's latest count. The other figures in the archive's sources are listed below.

On March 25, 1928, Hart and other Newhall cowboys set a stone in the Ruiz Cemetery "In memory of those who lost their lives in the Santa Clara flood Mar. 13, 1928 / Erected by the Newhall Cowboys."[18] The dam site was registered as California Historical Landmark No. 919 on April 26, 1978, and dedicated by the Santa Clarita Valley Historical Society on May 21; the state's plaque was placed at Power House No. 2 on November 5, 1979.[19][20] On March 12, 2019, the 91st anniversary, S.47 became law, and with it the Saint Francis Dam Disaster National Memorial and National Monument.[21][22]

The disaster has its own literature: Charles F. Outland's Man-Made Disaster: The Story of St. Francis Dam (1963, revised 1977); J. David Rogers's work in The St. Francis Dam Disaster Revisited (1995) and his paper for the American Society of Civil Engineers (2007); Norris Hundley Jr. and Donald C. Jackson's Heavy Ground (2015); and Jon Wilkman's Floodpath (2016).[23][24][25][26] Its primary record includes the transcript of the coroner's inquest and the jury's verdict of April 12, 1928, the Citizens' Restoration Committee's report on death and disability claims of July 15, 1929, and Stansell's roster and thesis.[27][28][29]

- Powerhouse No. 2: Pollack 2008 counts three survivors; Pollack's 2014 timeline says the flood 'claims the lives of 64 of the 67 workmen and their family members who lived nearby'; Stansell's 2014 table puts 73 in the PP2 community (63 BP&L, 10 BWWS). Not reconciled; the body gives only Pollack 2008's survivors.
- Pollack's 2014 extended timeline gives metric conversions in parentheses ('1-1/2 miles (2.4 km)', '18 mph (29 km/h)'), the style of an encyclopedia article. It may share text with an outside compilation; its figures not found elsewhere in the archive (64 of 67; wave heights) are treated here as leads, and the body does not rest on it except for the Kemp location disagreement.
- Ruiz family: six killed (Ruiz Cemetery record #2536; Pollack 2014) against eight Ruiz buried on March 18, 1928 (Leon Worden 2003, sg051703). HB1901 and the hub's 'Ruiz Family x7' are further leads.
- The Newhall morgue: Leon's captions (LW2715, FR2801) and his 2003 column treat the Masonic hall and the Hap-A-Lan dance hall as one building. The Reynolds dossier (inventory/review/jerry-reynolds-sources.md, H3 and RX1) marks Reynolds's 'Hap-A-Lan dance hall' as contradicted by the Newhall Signal of 1928 ('Masonic lodge'). If they are one building, the dossier verdict is wrong on that point. The body names only the Masonic hall at Railroad Avenue and Market Street, which both accounts support.
- Bodies at the Newhall morgue: Hart (May 8, 1928) 78 'at one time'; LW2715 'At least 78'; FR2801 'some 70 bodies'. The Newhall Signal of March 29 and April 5, 1928, quoted in sg051703, were not located as scans in the mirror for this draft.
- Harry Carey's Navajo employees: Photoplay May 1928, 'Sixty-four Navajo Indians, employed at the trading post, had left for their reservation'; LW2135, 'they went away that morning'; Pollack 2008, citing Harry Carey Jr., a month before. Not in the body.
- The Saugus Community Clubhouse, 23027 Drayton St., 'was utilized as an emergency shelter (per Ann Stansell 2014 PC) and possibly as a morgue (per Don Ray 2014 PC)' (LW2731). Personal communications only; not in the body.
- Edison Saugus substation: HS7067 quotes Wilkman 2016:94, 97 and Hundley & Jackson 2015:144 on the night crew and the destroyed cottages. The books are not in the archive; the body rests on the Newhall Land report instead.
- Construction dates and dimensions disagree across the captions (August 1924 start in the captions against 'December 1: Construction work begins (source: L.A. Times, 7-25-1926)' in the timeline; '600-foot-long, 185-foot-high' against '700-foot-long, 205-foot-high'). The body gives neither.
- 1928 newspaper pages naming the dam in their text but not their titles (nyt031528a-e, sfc031328, aen031328, bdn031428, nled031328, ombn031428) were not read for this draft; nled and the Los Angeles papers may carry Castaic Junction and Saugus detail.
- Harry Carey Ranch, place #599: its body says the ranch is 'seven miles up San Francisquito Canyon Road from the present Copper Hill Drive', the distance the captions give for the dam itself; the ranch was at the mouth of the canyon (LW2271, Carey #15919). Possibly a copied caption line.
- The Los Angeles Times negative-sleeve note quoted on LW2715 ('more than 600 residents') is a library's catalog note of unknown date, not a 1928 statement.
