## Draft body

John Boston is a newspaper columnist and local historian whose working life has been spent mostly at The Signal, as its sports editor, its columnist and, for a time, its editor.[1][2][3]

He was born Walt Cieplik. The archive's caption to a 1968 photograph of the Honby Men's Club at Hart High School names "Walt Cieplik (who later changed his name to John Boston)".[4] In his own account his mother named him "Walter Stanislav Cieplik Jr."[1]

By his own account, he first wrote for The Signal as a high school stringer in the mid-1960s. In 1970 NBC hired him to write news copy for VCCT, its new cable television outpost. In 1971 he began writing three stories a week, at $15 each, for his friend Phil Lanier, then The Signal's sports editor, and three years later he was sports editor himself.[1][2] He writes that he was briefly the paper's society editor in the mid-1970s under the name Count Vladimir Sauguslavsky. He also writes that he won his first writing award, for best column, at the Los Angeles Press Club Awards in 1977, and that he was fired and came back more than once. In the 1990s, he says, he became The Signal's daily columnist on page A2.[1][2]

By 2000 he was writing the Time Ranger column for The Signal's Sunday paper and teaching Santa Clarita Valley history through Golden Oak Adult School, and The Signal called him "Mr. SCV".[5] He was The Signal's editor in January 2005, when he signed the paper's obituary of Frederick Bailey Haskell "By John Boston, Editor".[3] A year later he shared the byline on its obituary of George Harris, Hart High School's first full-time principal, as one of two "Signal Staff Writers".[6]

The National Society of Newspaper Columnists gave him first place for humor (newspapers under 100,000 circulation) in 2000 and again in 2009, and honorable mentions in 2001 (sports) and 2010 (blog columns, for thebostonreport.net).[7] In 2006 the society gave him its Will Rogers Humanitarian Award, "for his efforts in behalf of readers of The Signal".[8]

He wrote "Santa Clarita Valley" (Arcadia Publishing, Images of America, 2009) with the Santa Clarita Valley Historical Society, and the novel "Naked Came the Sasquatch" (1993).[9] Philip Scorza's SCVTV history program "Points of Interest" featured him in an episode on the history of The Signal and in a special on growing up in Newhall in the 1960s.[5]

The archive holds six of his essays on the valley's history, written between 2000 and 2003: the three parts of "Laying Down the Law in Early Santa Clarita", "Shake, Rattle & Roll: SCV's Earthquake History", one on the death of Alex Mentry, and an ode to Ruth Newhall on her 90th birthday.[10]
